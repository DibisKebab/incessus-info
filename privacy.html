Privacy policy

This page explains what the Incessus app collects, where it goes and what choices you have.

Incessus is an iPhone app for people taking part in a research study on walking after brain surgery. The study is run at the National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery, Queen Square, London.

This policy covers the app. The participant information sheet that the research team gives you covers the study as a whole.

In short

  • The app reads Apple Health data on your iPhone, and only the kinds you allow.
  • When you press Send my data, it sends that data to the research team’s computer at the hospital, over an encrypted connection.
  • Your data is labelled with your study code, not your name.
  • The app never changes anything in Apple Health.
  • It has no adverts and does no tracking.
  • You can stop at any time.

Who is responsible

The app is published by Ibrahim Mansour, a member of the research team, at University College London.

The research team is based at the National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery and the UCL Queen Square Institute of Neurology.

For patients in the study, the participant information sheet names the organisation that is legally responsible for your information (the “data controller”), the legal basis for using it, and how long it is kept. You are given that sheet before you agree to take part.

Contact details are at the end of this page.

Who the app is for

The app is for adults who have agreed to take part in the study and have been given a study code by the research team. It is not meant for children.

Without a study code, the app sends no Health data.

Before the study opens to patients, members of the research team and their colleagues test the app with their own data, using test codes.

What the app collects

Your study code

This is the code the research team gave you. It is not your name.

The app sends the code to the research team’s computer when you press Continue, to check that it is on the study list. It also sends the code with everything else you send.

Your Apple Health data

Your iPhone asks you which kinds of Health data the app may read. The app can read only the kinds you switch on.

None of your Health data leaves your iPhone until you press Send my data.

The first time, the app sends the data your iPhone holds from 1 January 2020 onwards. After that, it sends only what is new, and it tells the research team which records you have since deleted from Apple Health.

The app asks to read the kinds of data below. It can only send what your iPhone actually holds.

  • Walking and movement: walking speed, step length, walking asymmetry, double support time, walking steadiness, stair speed, six-minute walk distance, steps, distance, flights climbed and falls.
  • Activity and exercise: energy used, exercise and stand time, daily activity summaries, workouts (the type, length and totals, but no map or route), measurements from running, cycling, swimming and other sports, and wheelchair pushes and distance.
  • Heart: heart rate, resting and walking heart rate, heart rate variability and recovery, beat-to-beat timings, blood pressure, cardio fitness, heart rhythm alerts, time in atrial fibrillation, and ECG results (the result and average heart rate, not the trace).
  • Breathing and body measurements: breathing rate, blood oxygen, lung function values, inhaler use, body temperature, wrist temperature during sleep, height, weight, body fat and waist size.
  • Sleep: sleep times and stages, and breathing disturbances during sleep.
  • Food, drink and blood sugar: nutrition records including water and caffeine, alcoholic drinks, blood alcohol, blood glucose and insulin.
  • Sound and light: headphone and background sound levels, time in daylight and UV exposure.
  • Symptoms and wellbeing: symptoms you have logged, such as headache, dizziness, tiredness, pain or memory lapses; mood and state-of-mind entries; mindful minutes; handwashing and toothbrushing.
  • Reproductive and sexual health: menstrual cycle records, ovulation and pregnancy test results, pregnancy, breastfeeding, contraception, menopause and sexual activity.
  • Other measurements: a few further readings that Apple Health can hold, such as skin conductance, blood flow at the wrist, and water depth and temperature when swimming or diving.
  • Your Health profile: your year of birth (not the full date), sex, blood type, skin type and whether you use a wheelchair.

Some of this is very personal. You choose. On the Health permission screen you can leave any switch off. You can change the switches later in Settings.

Details sent with each measurement

Each record is sent with:

  • its date and time, its value and its unit;
  • Apple Health’s own reference number for the record;
  • which app recorded it, and that app’s version;
  • the model and software version of the device that recorded it, such as an iPhone or Apple Watch model;
  • a fixed set of technical labels, such as whether a value was typed in by hand, the time zone, or the weather during a workout. A few labels can contain words written by other apps, such as the name of a food or a workout brand.

Before anything is sent, the app removes the names of your devices, which often include your own name. It also removes serial numbers and other hardware identifiers. Each of your devices is given a short code in their place.

Technical information about each send

With each send, the app also sends a short technical summary: how many records of each kind were sent, the dates they cover, any problems reading Apple Health, how long the send took, how much data was sent, the app’s version and your iPhone’s iOS version.

What the app does not collect

  • Your name, address, email address, phone number or any password.
  • Your location, or the routes of your workouts.
  • Your contacts, photos, camera or microphone.
  • Clinical health records, medications, hearing tests or vision prescriptions.
  • Any advertising identifier.

Where your data goes

Your Health data goes to one place: a computer run by the research team at the hospital.

It travels over an encrypted connection (HTTPS). On that computer it is kept on an encrypted disk, in a folder named with your study code.

The computer’s activity log records your study code, the time and the amount of data. It does not record your Health values or your internet address.

On its way to the hospital computer, the connection passes through a relay service called Tailscale. The relay forwards the encrypted data and cannot read it. Like any internet service, it can see your phone’s internet address and how much data is sent.

What stays on your iPhone

The app does not keep a copy of your Health measurements.

It keeps these things on your iPhone:

  • Your study code, and the dates when you agreed, allowed Health access and last sent data. These are kept in the iPhone’s secure keychain, on this device only.
  • Small files that record how far the last send got, so that the next send can carry on from there.
  • If the final “all done” message of a send could not be delivered, the technical summary of that send. It includes your year of birth, sex, blood type, skin type and wheelchair use. It is removed once it has been delivered.

These small files are in the app’s own folder. Your iPhone includes that folder in its backups, including iCloud Backup if you use it. The app has no iCloud storage of its own.

Deleting the app removes its files. iOS may keep the keychain entry, which is read again only if the app is put back on the same iPhone. The research team can reset the app to remove it.

Checking for updates

Each time you open the app or come back to it, it asks an update service whether a newer version of the app is available. The service is run by Expo (650 Industries, Inc.), a company in the United States.

That request contains a random number that identifies this copy of the app, and the app’s version details. Once after the app has failed to start, it also contains a short description of the problem. Expo also sees your phone’s internet address.

The request never contains your study code or any Health data.

Expo says that it keeps the random number and the internet address with its records of requests, deletes problem descriptions after three days, and does not use this information for advertising or share it with data brokers.

How your data is used

Your data is used for the research study described in your participant information sheet.

It is not used for advertising or marketing. It is not sold.

The app gives no medical advice and makes no diagnosis. It is not a way to tell the research team or your doctor that you are unwell. If you are worried about your health, speak to your own doctor or nurse.

Other organisations

The app gives your Health data to no other organisation. Your participant information sheet explains whether and how the research team shares study data with other researchers.

  • Expo receives only the update request described above.
  • Tailscale carries the encrypted connection and cannot read it.
  • Apple provides Apple Health, the App Store and TestFlight. Apple may collect its own information when you download or use the app, under Apple’s privacy policy.
  • GitHub hosts this web page and records visitors’ internet addresses for security.

How long your data is kept

The app itself keeps no copy of your Health measurements.

The research team keeps the data you send for as long as the study needs it. Your participant information sheet gives the exact period and says what happens to the data afterwards.

Your choices

Choose what to share
On your iPhone, open Settings, then Privacy & Security, then Health, then Incessus. Switch any kind of data on or off.
Stop a send
Press Stop. Nothing already sent is lost, and nothing more is sent until you press the Send button again.
Stop the app reading your Health data
Switch off its Health access in Settings, or delete the app.
Leave the study
Tell the research team. You do not have to give a reason.
Ask for your data to be deleted
Email the research team at the address below. They will tell you what can be deleted. Your participant information sheet explains what happens to data that has already been used in the research.

Your rights

UK data protection law gives you rights over your personal information. They include the right to be told how it is used, to ask for a copy, to ask for mistakes to be corrected, and to complain.

In research, some rights can be limited so that the results stay reliable. Your participant information sheet explains this.

To use your rights, contact the research team. If you are not happy with the answer, you can complain to the Information Commissioner’s Office at ico.org.uk or on 0303 123 1113.

Changes to this policy

If the app changes what it collects or where it sends it, this page will be updated and the date at the bottom will change.
